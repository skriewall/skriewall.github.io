# Project Overview  <a name="overview-main"></a>

### Context <a name="overview-context"></a>

Earlier in the year, our client ran a campaign to promote their new "Delivery Club" --- an initiative that costs a customer $100 per year for membership, but offers free grocery deliveries rather than the normal cost of $10 per delivery. They want to know if customers who joined the club have increased their spending in the three months following the campaign. The client hypothesizes that if customers are not paying for deliveries, they may shop more frequently and possibly purchase more for each transaction.

The aim of this project is to quantify the uplift in sales for customers that joined the club compared to what they would have spent if the Delivery Club had not come into existence.

<br>

### Actions <a name="overview-actions"></a>

We applied Causal Impact Analysis (full details below) using the *pycausalimpact* library.

In the client database, there is a *campaign_data* table that shows us which customers received each type of "Delivery Club" mailer, which customers were in the control group (receiving no mailer), and which customers did or did not join the club.

Delivery Club membership was open to *all customers*. The control group in the *campaign_data* table would help us measure the impact of *contacting* customers, but we wanted to measure the overall impact on sales from the Delivery Club itself. Therefore, we instead used customers who did not sign up as the control group. We believe customers who did not sign up should continue their normal shopping habits after the club went live. This helped us create the *counterfactual* (definition below) for the customers that did sign up.

Sales data was from the *transactions* table and was aggregated from a customer/transaction/product area level to customer/date level as per the requirements of the algorithm.

We used a three-month pre-period for the algorithm to model, and a three-month post-period for the counterfactual.

<br>

### Results <a name="overview-results"></a>

We saw a 41.1% uplift in sales in the three month post-period for those customers that joined the Delivery Club, compared to what we believe they would have spent had the club not been in existence. The uplift was deemed to be statistically significant at the 95% level.

<br>

### Growth and Next Steps <a name="overview-growth"></a>

We could look at this pool of customers (both those who did and did not join the Delivery club) and investigate whether there were any differences in sales in these time periods *last year*. This would be a way to check whether any of the uplift we see here is actually the result of seasonality, rather than a result of the Delivery Club launching.

It would be interesting to continue to track this uplift over time and see whether:

* It continues to grow
* It flattens or returns to normal
* We see any form of uplift pull-forward

It would also be interesting to analyze what the uplift consists of. Are customers increasing their spending across the same categories, or are they buying more in specific or new categories?

# Causal Impact Analysis Overview  <a name="causal-impact-overview"></a>

### Context

One of the most common tasks undertaken in Data Science and Data Analysis is understanding and quantifying a change in a key business metric after some event has taken place. Depending on the industry, this could be the uplift in sales after a promotion or a product release; the additional clicks, conversions, or signups generated by an online ad campaign; or the change in share price after a market event.

Whatever the scenario, the task is essentially the same --- to understand how big this change was. In order to reliably estimate the true impact of an event, we need some way of understanding what *would have happened* if the event had not taken place.

In most cases the trends *preceding the event in question* isn’t tame, it is filled with lumps and bumps and ups and downs. When some key event does take place, understanding what *would have happened had the event not taken place* so we can estimate the true impact can be difficult!

In some cases, the event being analyzed is part of a randomized and controlled experiment, which means comparing the difference between the group that was affected by the event and a control group that was purposely held back from the effect of the event. However, there are many cases where a randomized, controlled experiment cannot be run, due to expense or because it is simply impossible to control the variables. For example, in the case of measuring the change in a share price after an event, we do not have a direct control group to lean on for comparison purposes.

Causal Impact Analysis is an approach that works well whether we have a randomized and controlled experiment or not.

<br>

### How It Works

Causal Impact is a time-series technique originally developed by Google. It estimates what *would have happened* (known as a "counterfactual") by applying a model to comparable data in a pre-period and projecting the model onto the data in a post-period. The difference between the actual data and the counterfactual in the post-period is the estimated impact of the event.

The *comparable data* that we pass in to the algorithm can be a control group, another set of related data, or multiple sets of related data. For this approach to work robustly and reliably, the comparable data must adhere to several rules:

It must not be affected by the event, but it must be predictive of the output or have some relationship with the initial time-series data. In the case of a randomized experiment, we could use the control group as our additional set of data. In the case of no randomized experiment with control group, we need to find other sets of data that meet the requirements. The other data must not be affected by the event, but should have some relationship or correlation with the time-series data we are measuring. For example, if we were trying to measure stock prices, we could use other stocks that are in a similar industry to us.

The additional "comparable" data provides the algorithm insights into the trends of the data over time. The algorithm uses these insights to model the relationship between the two (or more) time-series in the pre-period --- it finds a set of rules that best predict the time-series of interest, based on the movements and fluctuations of the other time-series that we provide.

Once the algorithm has modeled this relationship, it applies this model in the post-period resulting in an estimation for the counterfactual, or what *would have happened* to the time series if our event never took place, according to the model.

Given the counterfactual, we can proceed to calculate the estimation for the effect caused by the event.

<br>

### Application

We will use a Python library called *pycausalimpact* to apply the Causal Impact algorithm to the data. The algorithm will model the relationships. The library also provides very useful plots and a summary to interpret the results.

# Data Overview and Preparation  <a name="causal-impact-data-prep"></a>


In the client database, there is a *campaign_data* table that shows us which customers received each type of "Delivery Club" mailer, which customers were in the control group (receiving no mailer), and which customers did or did not join the club.

Delivery Club membership was open to *all customers*. The control group in the *campaign_data* table would help us measure the impact of *contacting* customers, but we want to measure the overall impact on sales from the Delivery Club itself. Therefore, we instead use customers who did not sign up as the control group. We believe customers who did not sign up should continue their normal shopping habits after the club went live. This will help us create the counterfactual for the customers that did sign up.

In the code below, we:

* Import the necessary Python libraries
* Import the required data from the *transactions* and *campaign_data* tables (includes data from 3 months pre- and 3 months post-campaign)
* Aggregate the *transactions* table from customer/transaction/product area level to customer/date level
* Merge with the signup flag from the *campaign_data* table
* Pivot and aggregate the data to get it in the form of aggregated daily sales split out by signup groups
* Reformat the data for the pycausalimpact algorithm
* Give the groups some meaningful names to help with interpretation

```python
# Import required packages
from causalimpact import CausalImpact
import pandas as pd

# Import data tables
transactions = ...
campaign_data = ...

# Aggregate transactions data by customer & date
customer_daily_sales = transactions.groupby(['customer_id', 'transaction_date'])['sales_cost'].sum().reset_index()

# Merge in the signup flag
customer_daily_sales = pd.merge(customer_daily_sales, campaign_data, how = 'inner', on = 'customer_id')

# Pivot the data to aggregate daily sales by signup group
causal_impact_df = customer_daily_sales.pivot_table(index = 'transaction_date',
                                                    columns = 'signup_flag',
                                                    values = 'sales_cost',
                                                    aggfunc = 'mean')

# Provide a frequency (day) for the DateTimeIndex to avoid warning message
causal_impact_df.index.freq = 'D'

# We want the impacted group (signup_flag = 1) in the first column - swap columns
causal_impact_df = causal_impact_df[[1, 0]]

# Rename columns to something more meaningful
causal_impact_df.columns = ['member', 'non_member']
```

# Applying The Causal Impact Algorithm <a name="causal-impact-fit"></a>

The code below specifies the start and end dates of the "pre-period" and "post-period". It applies the algorithm by passing in the DataFrame and the specified pre- and post-period time windows.

The algorithm models the relationship between members and non-members in the pre-period, then uses this to create the counterfactual, i.e., what would happen to the average daily spending for members in the post-period if they had continued on as non-members.

The difference between this counterfactual and the actual data in the post-period is the "causal impact".

```python
# Define the pre- and post-periods
pre_period = ['2020-04-01', '2020-06-30']
post_period = ['2020-07-01', '2020-09-30']

# Apply the algorithm
ci = CausalImpact(causal_impact_df, pre_period, post_period)
```

We can use the created *ci* object to examine and plot the results.

# Analyzing The Results <a name="causal-impact-results"></a>

### Plotting The Results

The *pycausalimpact* library makes plotting the results extremely easy with a single line of code, as below:

```python
# Plot the impact
ci.plot()
```

The resulting plots can be seen below.

![Causal Impact Results Plot](/img/posts/causal-impact-results-plot.png "Causal Impact Results Plot")

The vertical dotted line down the middle of each plot is the date that the Delivery Club membership started. Everything to the left of this dotted line is the pre-period, and everything to the right of the dotted line is the post-period.

**Chart 1:  Actual vs. Counterfactual**

The top chart shows the actual data for the impacted group (the *actual* average daily sales for customers who went on to sign up to the Delivery Club) as a solid black line. It also shows the counterfactual, with the dotted blue line. The purple area around the blue dotted line is the confidence interval around the counterfactual (i.e., the range the prediction should fall in). A wider confidence interval suggests that the model is less sure about the counterfactual prediction. All of this is taken into account when calculating the actual uplift.

Looking at the first chart, there appears to be an increase in average daily spending for customers who joined the club compared to what it would have been if the club never came into existence. We will look at the actual numbers for this farther below.

**Chart 2:  Pointwise Effects**

The middle chart shows for each data point in the time-series the *raw differences* between the actual values and the values for the counterfactual. It is plotting the *differences* from Chart 1. For example, if the actual and the counterfactual were the same on Day 1, this chart would show a value of 0. If the actual is higher than the counterfactual the plotted point would be a positive value on this chart. It is essentially showing how far above or below the counterfactual the actual values are.

For the pre-period we see a difference oscillating around 0, but in the post-period there are mostly positive values. This aligns with Chart 1, where the actual average spending was greater than the counterfactual.

**Chart 3:  Cumulative Effects**

The bottom chart shows the cumulative uplift over time in the post-period, essentially adding up the Pointwise contributions from the second chart over time. This helps the viewer get a feel for what the total uplift or difference is at any point in time.

Again, there appears to be a cumulative uplift over time.

At the top left of the results summary (above) we see that in the post-period the average actual daily sales per customer over the post-period was $171. This is higher than that of the counterfactual, which was $121. The counterfactual prediction had a 95% confidence interval of approximately ($113, $130).

In the middle left side, the summary also calculates the *absolute effect*, which is the difference between actual and counterfactual (i.e., the difference between $171 and $121). This is the average daily *uplift* in sales over the post-period. It also gives the confidence intervals surrounding that effect. Since 0.00 does not fall in the interval (41.56, 58.54), we can confidently say that there *was* an uplift driven by the Delivery Club.

On the bottom left, the percentage uplift is calculated along with a confidence interval.

On the right side of the summary, we see the *cumulative* values for these across the entire post-period, rather than the average per day.

We can also use the *pycausalimpact* library to give us this same information as a written output, like a report:

```python
# Results summary in written/report form
print(ci.summary(output = 'report'))

>> Analysis report {CausalImpact}

During the post-intervention period, the response variable had an average value of approx. 171.33. By contrast, in the absence of an intervention, we would have expected an average response of 121.42.

The 95% interval of this counterfactual prediction is [112.79, 129.77].

Subtracting this prediction from the observed response yields an estimate of the causal effect the intervention had on the response variable. This effect is 49.92 with a 95% interval of [41.56, 58.54]. For a discussion of the significance of this effect, see below.

Summing up the individual data points during the post-intervention period (which can only sometimes be meaningfully interpreted), the response variable had an overall value of 15762.67. By contrast, had the intervention not taken place, we would have expected a sum of 11170.19. The 95% interval of this prediction is [10376.65, 11938.77].

The above results are given in terms of absolute numbers. In relative terms, the response variable showed an increase of +41.11%. The 95% interval of this percentage is [34.23%, 48.22%].

This means that the positive effect observed during the intervention period is statistically significant and unlikely to be due to random fluctuations. It should be noted, however, that the question of whether this increase also bears substantive significance can only be answered by comparing the absolute effect (49.92) to the original goal
of the underlying intervention.

The probability of obtaining this effect by chance is very small (Bayesian one-sided tail-area probability p = 0.0). This means the causal effect can be considered statistically
significant.
```

This is the same information as in the summary, but put into a written report which can go straight to the client.

To conclude, there was an uplift of 41.11% in sales for customers that joined the Delivery Club above what we believe they would have spent without the club. The uplift was deemed to be statistically significant.
